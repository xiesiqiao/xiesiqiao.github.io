The United States mid-life mortality crisis is marked by deep and persistent geographic disparities, confirming the National Academies' call to investigate how "place matters" for population health (NASEM, 2021). It remains unclear whether this crisis is best explained by hyperlocal neighborhood capacity or by broader regional economic shocks. This paper addresses this question by classifying neighborhood-level mortality patterns into seven dominant, multi-year trajectories (2005-2021) using a machine-learning sequence-analysis technique, then partitioning the contribution of local, spatial, and macroeconomic forces to these trajectories with a multi-scalar spatial multinomial logit model and Shapley variance decomposition. Mortality risk is overwhelmingly local: the characteristics of a tract and its immediate neighbors account for nearly 90% of the explained variance in mortality trajectories, compared to just 10% for commuting-zone and state-level conditions combined. Within this local context, accumulated community wealth and educational attainment provide the strongest protection, not income or employment — a one-standard-deviation increase in local college education raises the probability of a resilient trajectory by 15 percentage points. The highest-risk trajectories cluster in neighborhoods embedded in racially segregated surroundings: a high concentration of Black residents among neighboring tracts substantially lowers the likelihood of a low-risk trajectory, independent of a tract's own demographics. Because mortality risk tracks a community's sustained exposure to this localized, compounding geography of wealth, education, and segregation — not a single point in time — reversing the crisis will require sustained investment in neighborhood-level wealth and educational infrastructure, not regional stimulus alone.
