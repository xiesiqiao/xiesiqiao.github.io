Since the 1990s the U.S. has experienced a striking increase in the mortality of the working-age population, driven largely by deaths from drugs, alcohol, and suicide, and exacerbated by the COVID-19 pandemic. To understand these trends and target interventions toward the highest-risk populations, it is essential to map these dynamics at a granular scale. While regional patterns are well documented, underlying neighborhood-level dynamics remain unknown, largely due to a lack of high-resolution data. This gap is critical: relying on coarse county-level averages or static snapshots obscures the structural pathways driving health disparities, leading to inferential errors and misallocated interventions. This paper addresses this gap by assembling a first-of-its-kind neighborhood-scale dataset, harmonizing six million death certificates across 13 states from 2005 to 2021, and developing a sequence-analysis framework to track mid-life mortality at an unprecedented neighborhood resolution. The analysis reveals seven distinct local mortality trajectories that are highly differentiated across and within major regions and closely connected to sociodemographic composition — for example, resilient neighborhoods occur across diverse educational attainments, whereas adverse mortality trajectories concentrate in older communities with lower average educational attainment. These trajectories show that mortality risk is not merely an expression of broad regional trends, but follows distinct local pathways dictated by unique local structural conditions that aggregate county-level statistics consistently obscure — evidence needed to design place-based health interventions tailored to each community's structural drivers of mortality.
